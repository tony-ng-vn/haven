Let us look at what is true, what is false, and what is uncertain about creating Love Alarm style device detection today.

What is true: A purely software based app running on two standard smartphones in their pockets, scanning and advertising continuously with high precision in the background, is blocked by Apple and Google operating system architecture. You cannot write standard iOS code that turns a locked phone into a continuous background Bluetooth beacon for other phones without the OS throttling or killing it.

What is false: The idea that background phone to phone Bluetooth is the only way to create the Love Alarm experience.

What is uncertain: Whether your initial users actually care about passive continuous background scanning, or if they just care about the emotional moment when someone who matches them is nearby.

If you are committed to making this exist, you essentially have three different paths to invent around the platform wall. Each path forces a radically different trade off on your startup.

First, you can invent around the software by building physical hardware. If phones refuse to advertise reliably in the background, a dedicated wearable like a smart pin, ring, or keyfob will advertise continuously without operating system restrictions. The phone then acts as the receiver, or the tokens communicate directly. This bypasses Apple background advertising rules entirely. But ask yourself: Are you ready to take on hardware manufacturing, distribution, and unit economics just to test if people want the social experience?

Second, you can use hybrid system capabilities. You can combine background location triggers with server side distance calculations, or leverage active system states like Live Activities to keep spatial scanning alive when a user enters a social venue. It gives you room level proximity rather than exact sub meter Bluetooth precision, but it works on existing devices today. Is exact ten meter precision mandatory for the core user emotion, or is knowing you are both in the same coffee shop enough?

Third, you can invent a new interaction ritual. Love Alarm in the drama relied on passive scanning, but what if the magic comes from active intent? What if users tap an activate radar button when they step into a bar, triggering a short, high precision scanning window while the app is active?

As a founder, your biggest risk right now is not whether Bluetooth physics can be hacked. Your biggest risk is spending six months inventing a complex technical bypass before proving that users actually want the core social outcome.

You can build something that feels like Love Alarm. The difficult version is:
Detect every Haven user within roughly 10 meters, instantly, while both phones are locked and the app has not been opened recently.
A normal iPhone app cannot promise that. iOS allows background Bluetooth modes, but background advertisements are reduced, discoveries are combined, and scan intervals become longer. Apple describes Bluetooth advertising as “best effort.” (Apple Developer)
But there are several versions you can build.
1. The realistic phone-only Haven MVP
Create an intentional Radar Mode:
    1    The user activates “Visible for the next 15 minutes.”
    2    Haven advertises a common Bluetooth service identifier.
    3    Nearby Haven phones scan for that identifier.
    4    After discovering each other, they exchange rotating anonymous IDs.
    5    Your server determines whether there is a relevant connection.
    6    The phone produces the alarm animation, vibration, or notification.
When both apps are foregrounded—or at least actively being used—this can work well. Apple’s Multipeer Connectivity framework is specifically designed to discover and communicate with nearby instances of an app using Bluetooth and peer-to-peer networking. (Apple Developer)
You could design it around moments where users naturally activate it:
    •    Entering an event
    •    Walking into a café
    •    Attending a conference
    •    Joining a university social space
    •    Starting “available to meet” mode
That is still a real Love Alarm experience. It just is not secretly operating with perfect reliability every second of the day.
2. The hybrid version I would build first
Do not make Bluetooth responsible for the entire system.
Use several increasingly precise signals:
Same city
   ↓
Same venue or geographic zone
   ↓
Both users currently active
   ↓
Bluetooth encounter detected
   ↓
Sustained strong Bluetooth signal
   ↓
Trigger Haven alarm
Location or venue participation narrows the candidates. Bluetooth confirms that two devices were likely physically close. This avoids continuously searching for every phone in the world.
For example, at a startup event:
    1    Tony joins the event’s Haven room.
    2    Twenty other people join.
    3    The backend knows those users might be nearby.
    4    Radar Mode only looks for anonymous IDs belonging to that room.
    5    When a relevant ID remains nearby for several seconds, Haven triggers.
This architecture also makes false-positive prevention, privacy controls, blocking, and consent much easier.
3. The actual invention: a Haven wearable
If you want continuous, dependable detection, create a small physical device:
    •    A pin
    •    Bracelet
    •    Keychain
    •    Card
    •    Ring
    •    Clip attached to a phone case
Each Haven Beacon would:
    1    Continuously broadcast rotating anonymous Bluetooth IDs.
    2    Continuously listen for other Haven Beacons.
    3    Measure approximate signal strength.
    4    Record encounters locally.
    5    Vibrate or glow immediately when conditions match.
    6    Synchronize encounter data with the Haven app later.

       ↓                              ↓
                  Haven backend
The crucial difference is that your hardware controls its background execution. Apple cannot suspend the beacon’s processor the way it suspends an iPhone app.
A prototype could be made with an inexpensive low-power Bluetooth microcontroller. After proving the experience, you could investigate UWB for more accurate ranging. Apple officially supports interactions between iPhones and third-party UWB accessories through Nearby Interaction, although developing compatible accessories involves Apple’s accessory specifications and approved hardware solutions. (Apple Developer)
However, Apple’s phone-to-phone Nearby Interaction is not itself passive stranger discovery: the apps must first exchange discovery tokens, and Apple describes the distance-and-direction interaction as running while the app is in the foreground. (Apple Developer)
What I would not attempt
I would not rely on:
    •    Playing silent audio to keep Haven alive
    •    Pretending to be a navigation or fitness app
    •    Abusing VoIP background execution
    •    Keeping the microphone continuously active for ultrasound
    •    Wi-Fi MAC-address tracking
    •    Trying to use Find My as a general nearby-user network
These would be unreliable, privacy-sensitive, or likely to create App Store review problems. Apple explicitly says background services may only be used for their intended purposes. (Apple Developer)
The strongest path for Haven
Build it in stages:
Stage 1 — Active Radar
Phone-only, opt-in, app open, rotating IDs, Bluetooth scanning. Test whether people actually enjoy the alarm moment.
Stage 2 — Social spaces
Event rooms, café zones, campus areas, parties and conferences. Use check-in plus Bluetooth confirmation.
Stage 3 — Imperfect background encounters
Experiment with legitimate Core Bluetooth background modes. Treat detections as opportunistic—not guaranteed—and never promise instant detection.
Stage 4 — Haven Beacon
Only after proving demand, develop a wearable that performs continuous proximity detection independently of the phone.
So yes, you can make Love Alarm. The first product should not be “magically detect everyone everywhere.” It should be:
Turn on Haven Radar and discover when a meaningful person is physically near you.
Then, if that moment is valuable enough, the hardware becomes the invention that removes Apple’s ceiling.
